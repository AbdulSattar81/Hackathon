# Problem Statement

## The gap
In a U.S. emergency room, a patient who doesn't speak English waits an average of **several minutes to over an hour** for a qualified medical interpreter — by phone, video cart, or in person. In an ER, minutes decide outcomes. During that wait, clinicians improvise with hand gestures, a bilingual relative, or a child translating for a parent. Each of those is a documented source of medical error.

Language barriers are not an edge case. Patients with limited English proficiency (LEP) have **longer stays, higher readmission rates, more adverse events, and lower satisfaction** than English-speaking patients with the same conditions. The failure mode is specific: the *moment* of communication — "Where's the pain?", "Are you allergic to anything?", "We need to do this now" — passes before the interpreter arrives.

## Why Houston
Houston is one of the most linguistically diverse cities in the U.S., with roughly **145 languages** spoken across the metro. The Texas Medical Center — the largest medical complex in the world — sees this every shift. No interpreter pool covers 145 languages on demand, 24/7, instantly. That structural gap is exactly where patients fall through.

## What's missing in existing tools
Generic translation apps fail in the ER for three reasons:
1. **They're flat.** A robotic monotone strips the urgency from "we need to move now" and the reassurance from "you're going to be okay." Tone *is* clinical information. A frightened patient reads the room from how something is said, not just what.
2. **They have no patient context.** They don't know this patient's allergies, prior visits, or that "Lisinopril" should be explained as a blood-pressure medication, not transliterated as a brand name.
3. **They leave no signal.** Hospitals can't see which language gaps are quietly driving readmissions, so they can't staff against them.

## Our solution: Interpreter
A real-time ER translator where the **doctor speaks English and the patient instantly hears their native language in a calm voice that preserves the urgency and emotion** of the original. It removes the "wait for interpreter" delay at the bedside, carries tone (not just words), pulls per-patient context, and turns every consult into anonymized analytics that tell the hospital where its language gaps actually hurt.

## Who it's for
ER physicians, nurses, and triage staff at high-diversity hospitals — starting with the TMC — and the LEP patients who currently get a worse version of emergency care because the words arrive too late.
